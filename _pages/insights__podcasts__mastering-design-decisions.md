# Product design decisions podcast: how product, engineering and design leaders agree

By [Jatin Leuva](/insights/authors/jatin-leuva) · Published 16 July 2026 · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read

Product decisions rarely come with one complete source of truth. Customer feedback is often partial, and analytics can show what people did without saying why. Engineers know what is feasible and what it will cost to run. Business leaders carry commitments the company has already made. Designers have to keep the user, and the product the team is heading towards, in view among all of that.

Jatin Leuva, Founder and CEO of Tcules, hosts three guests: Parasharan Chari, Founder of Medkart; Dhimant Trivedi, Head of Engineering at Contrado; and Akshay Vyas, Creative Head at Knovos. This page summarises the conversation in writing; the recording is linked below.

[Watch the recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=44Nta0CkcOY).

**Key takeaways**

- Jatin Leuva hosts leaders from Medkart, Contrado and Knovos on how product decisions get made together.
- Each feedback route tells a different story, so keep track of where each piece came from.
- Data can strengthen or weaken an explanation. People still have to find out what caused an event.
- Roadmap decisions are negotiations, and a responsible partial release records the compromise and the trigger for the next stage.
- AI makes options cheaper to generate, and people still own the judgement and its effect on customers.

## Product research and constraints: start with how you listen {#the-first-decision-is-how-to-listen}

The speakers describe several ways a product team can hear from customers: direct research, customer-service questions, QA feedback, input from the product team and stakeholders, and product data.

Each route tells a different story. A support ticket exists because a problem was serious enough to report. A product metric shows what happened and leaves out why. A stakeholder requirement may stand for revenue, regulation, service or one person's request.

The practical lesson is to keep track of where each piece of feedback came from:

- who saw the issue;
- which user and situation it represents;
- whether it was seen directly or passed on;
- what actually happened, kept apart from any feature someone proposes;
- what would challenge the interpretation.

## Data can test an explanation; people still have to find one {#data-validates-an-interpretation-it-does-not-exp}

One discussion concerns a feature that shows up often in usage data. High use can mean the feature is valuable. It can also mean people take an indirect route because the intended one is missing or hard to find.

Parasharan returns to it from the retail side and Dhimant from engineering. The point is the same from both: a metric records an event, and someone still has to find out what caused it. Data can strengthen or weaken an explanation. Understanding the situation remains the team's job.

## A worked example: medicine availability at a pharmacy counter {#medicine-availability-as-a-worked-example}

The recording describes a pharmacy where stock is spread across a store, nearby stores, a warehouse and a far larger range of medicines. A customer at the counter wants a dependable answer to one question: when can I get this medicine?

Showing more stock data goes only part of the way. The system has to show location, availability, how the order would be fulfilled and when, in a form the person at the counter can act on. The screen depends on how the business runs behind it.

## How product teams make roadmap decisions together {#roadmap-decisions-are-negotiated-constraints}

The participants describe roadmap work as a recurring negotiation between what is required, what it is worth, whether it is feasible, what it costs and how long it takes. Product managers may speak for stakeholders; designers and engineers bring different information.

When the intended experience runs ahead of what engineering can deliver today, a responsible partial release lets both sides keep what matters. It can:

- meet a current business or user need;
- keep a clear route to the intended product;
- record the compromise as a compromise, so nobody later mistakes it for the plan;
- define what evidence or capability will trigger the next stage.

The risk is a quick fix whose reason everyone forgets.

## Stakeholder disagreement in product design, and the record that helps {#stakeholder-disagreement-is-often-missing-transl}

The conversation questions the easy explanation that a stakeholder resists design out of ego. Often each side is missing part of the picture: a business leader may miss the product consequence of a design proposal, and a designer may miss its operational or commercial cost.

A better presentation helps. A shared decision record helps more:

| What to record | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Objective | What change is the organisation trying to create? |
| User evidence | What do we know about the work this affects? |
| Business consequence | What is the effect on revenue, cost, risk, service or strategy? |
| Technical constraint | What is impossible, expensive or unsafe today? |
| Options | Which paths are still open? |
| Accountable owner | Who makes the decision and carries the consequence? |
| How we will check | What will we observe after committing? |
| What we are putting off | What must not be lost from the future product? |

Small choices need only part of the record. The bigger the consequence, the more people should take part.

## Product, engineering and design collaboration: what earns a designer trust {#what-makes-a-designer-trustworthy-inside-an-orga}

Near the end, the discussion names four things a designer should understand: the product or service, the customer, how the company operates and what the team can do. A proposal gains credibility when it holds up against all four, and a designer can get there with a working knowledge of each.

The organisation owes designers something in return: a clearly framed need and access to the people and information behind it. Trust grows through the work, daily contact and understanding shown over time. A title only starts it.

## AI speeds up the work, and people still own it {#ai-changes-speed-not-responsibility}

The participants treat AI as an accelerator whose value depends on how well the practitioner can frame and judge the work. The recording includes concern that novices will hand over the fundamentals, and examples of AI assisting with coding, content, reporting and expression.

Generating options has become cheaper. The team still has to understand the requirements, judge the output and own its effect on customers and operations.

Tcules works to the same rule: people own the work, including work done with AI tools. See [How We Work](/how-we-work) and [Product Design](/services/product-design).

## Accountable product decisions: what to record when disciplines disagree {#what-should-the-team-record-when-disciplines-dis}

When disciplines disagree, write down the decision to be made and what each side's reading rests on. Add the constraints that cannot move, the options turned down and the owner accountable for the next step. The negotiation still happens. The record lets anyone review it later, so a roadmap choice is remembered as a choice the team made.

AI can produce more options and summaries quickly. Responsibility stays with the people who understand the consequence and can question how the problem was framed. Keep the original material within reach, mark what was generated and record who accepted the resulting choice.

The conversation suggests disagreement often comes from each side missing part of the picture, more than from ego. A shared record helps: the objective, user evidence, business consequence, technical constraint, options, accountable owner, how the team will check and what it is putting off.
