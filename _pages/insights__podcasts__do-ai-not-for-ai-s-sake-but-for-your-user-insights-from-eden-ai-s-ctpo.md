# Eden AI's Samy Melaine on building AI products for users: podcast

By [Jatin Leuva](/insights/authors/jatin-leuva) · Published 6 April 2026 · Updated 26 October 2026 · 6 min read

Jatin Leuva, Founder and CEO of Tcules, talks with Samy Melaine, Founder and CPTO of Eden AI, about building a developer platform in a fast-changing AI market. This page summarises the conversation in writing; the recording is linked below.

[Watch the recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QNz1zr3U7Zo).

**Key takeaways**

- Jatin Leuva talks with Samy Melaine, Founder and CPTO of Eden AI, about building a developer platform in a fast-changing AI market.
- Offering many models adds work for users unless the product makes the choice easier.
- At Eden AI, developers, product and design work on the same problem, moving between feedback, feasibility and usability.
- In a fast-moving AI market, a roadmap works best as a statement of direction, constraints and confidence.
- As a product reaches less experienced developers, choosing a model and building a workflow become experience problems.

## Choosing AI providers: model choice as a product problem {#model-choice-was-a-product-problem-before-it-was}

Eden AI exists because of a gap in the market. Developers could reach a growing number of AI models and providers, but comparing, connecting and running them meant repeating the same work. Samy describes a platform that brings the choice of provider, the linking of models into workflows and the monitoring of live use into one developer environment.

Offering many models matters because choice alone can add mental and operational work. The product has to make the choice easier. The user has to understand which model suits the job, connect models into a workflow, test how it behaves and see what happens in production.

## How Eden AI's engineers, product and design work on one problem {#technical-and-product-decisions-belong-in-the-sa}

Samy's CPTO role covers both technology and product. He explains that a developer tool has to be dependable and also make unfamiliar AI capability understandable. For him, design is part of building the technology: the team moves between user feedback, technical feasibility and usability.

The conversation points to one way of working:

1. A user problem or piece of feedback reaches the team.
2. Developers look at the product and at what is technically possible.
3. Product and design turn the idea into an interaction people can understand.
4. The proposal goes back to engineering to be challenged and built.
5. Use of the product produces the next round of feedback.

Samy adds that developers contribute product insight, because they use the developer-facing platform and take part in support.

## An AI product roadmap as a statement of direction {#the-roadmap-is-a-communication-tool}

The conversation resists the idea that a roadmap can predict a fast-moving AI market for six months with false confidence. A roadmap still earns its place when it tells people the current direction, the constraints and how confident the team is, instead of listing future features as if they were fixed.

For an AI product, that suggests each roadmap item should state:

- the user problem;
- what the technology can do today, and what is still uncertain;
- the technical dependency;
- how confident the team is in the proposed product response;
- what the team needs to learn before committing more widely.

## Designing AI workflow products for less experienced developers {#a-wider-audience-changes-what-control-has-to-loo}

A product built around its API can begin with technical reliability. As it reaches a wider range of developers, including people with less AI experience, choosing a model and building a workflow become experience problems for the product to solve.

The task is to explain the technical differences that matter and keep a route to the operational detail. A non-specialist may need a plain explanation of which model fits. An experienced developer may need latency, provider, cost, monitoring and failure information.

## What each theme means for a product team {#what-the-recording-supports-and-where-it-stops}

| Theme in the recording | What it means for the product |
|---|---|
| Many providers and models | Give users a way to compare models and link them together |
| API-first reliability | Keep live use stable |
| Developer participation in support | Bring what developers learn in support into product research |
| Roadmap as communication | Show direction and confidence openly |
| User problem before AI feature | Start from the user's work and its value |

## Building AI products for users: start from the work {#find-the-work-worth-improving-before-scoping-the}

Building AI around the user's work sits comfortably with technical ambition. The order is: find the work worth improving, understand what the technology makes possible, decide what the product will and will not do, and learn from use. Technical and design judgement move together.

The related [Eden AI case](/case-studies/eden-ai-workflow-builder-search-redesign) describes Tcules' design work on workflow creation and search. Developers could start a workflow from a template, by asking the AI or from scratch, and each route led somewhere slightly different. We brought them into one search box that recognises how a person wants to start, while keeping search and AI help as separate moves they can see. [AI Products and Developer Tools](/expertise/ai-products-developer-tools) covers the other AI and developer products we design.

## How to use this episode on your own roadmap {#how-to-use-this-episode}

The conversation makes the case for starting from the user's work and putting it ahead of model novelty, and for planning product and technical roadmaps together. For the controls an AI workflow needs, see [how much an AI feature should show to earn trust](/insights/building-trust-and-transparency-in-ai-first-user-interfaces).

After listening, try this on your own roadmap. Name one thing a user decides, the smallest job the model does to support it, and what happens when the model cannot help. If you cannot write those down, the roadmap item is still a capability label with no product commitment behind it.

## What controls should an AI workflow have? {#what-authority-and-recovery-controls-does-an-ai-}

Enforce authority at each point where the AI can act on something, as well as in the prompt and at the top of the workflow. Before it acts, show what it will affect, what it is based on and what permission it holds. Show the consequence too, and whether anyone has reviewed it. After it acts, show the result, including anything that failed halfway. Then give people a way to correct it, retry it, stop it or hand the work back to a person. How much control a step needs depends on its consequence and on how easily it can be undone.
