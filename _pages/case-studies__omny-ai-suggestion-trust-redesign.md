# Omny AI suggestion UX: people choose what reaches the listing

Omny AI, acquired by Primelis, generates product benefits and use cases for brands and agencies selling on Amazon. Unreviewed suggestions were entering the work with too much implied authority.

Users were losing trust when suggestions they had not properly reviewed turned out to be wrong. Tcules changed how those suggestions entered the work.

We redesigned the interaction so AI proposals stayed separate from committed content until a person reviewed and accepted them. Every line in a listing had a visible author, and nothing the AI wrote reached it until a person chose it.

## At a glance {#case-context}

| Product | Omny AI, Amazon seller software that uses AI to write product benefits and use cases, for brands and agencies. |
|---|---|
| Our role | AI interaction design, information architecture and control patterns for AI-written listing content. |
| The key question | How can AI suggestions reach a listing only after a person has reviewed and accepted them? |
| What we delivered | An interaction where AI suggestions stay visibly separate until the user chooses what goes into the listing, with purple for AI content and blue for the person's own. |

## The data patterns came first, then the screens {#data-first}

Before any interface work, Tcules examined the raw data patterns to find the relationships that mattered, so the structure came from the data itself.

Each element was then designed against the question a user would be asking at that point. How do I set a budget allocation? Which indicator belongs on this screen? What is this number being compared against?

## The problem: AI suggestions were accepted without a real review {#the-interface-had-quietly-changed-who-was-accoun}

The original design filled the content area with the AI’s suggestions before the person did anything. Accepting them required no deliberate choice. People could move ahead without giving the generated content the review it needed.

The Omny team's own description of it was exact: users were holding Omny accountable for suggestions they had not properly reviewed.

The default had made acceptance automatic. Tcules gave choosing a suggestion its own action, so users had a clear point at which to take responsibility for the content.

## Review and choose AI suggestions: an empty area and a deliberate click {#blank-by-default-and-a-click-to-accept}

Tcules reworked the interaction as a collaboration between the person and the AI:

- the final content area starts empty
- the suggestions sit in their own panel beside it
- adding one takes a deliberate click

The deliberate click gives acceptance a clear place in the workflow. The person chooses what belongs in the listing, with the suggested material still available beside it.

The wider lesson: where an AI feature makes someone accountable for its output, accepting a suggestion should take at least as much effort as reading it.

## Purple for AI, blue for people: who wrote each part of the listing {#purple-is-the-machine-blue-is-the-person}

The second change runs through the whole product. Tcules gave authorship a colour:

- purple for anything the AI generated, suggested or did automatically
- blue for anything a person typed, chose or overrode

The same distinction carried across budget allocation, the budget strategy timeline and product content management. Purple marked material the system extracted. Blue marked material a person added. Users could see authorship as they reviewed the content.

## After the AI suggestion trust redesign: what Omny reported {#what-the-work-establishes-and-what-it-does-not}

Separating suggestions from committed content gave users a clearer role in the result. Omny reported that users became more aware of how they were using the AI and more forgiving when a suggestion was wrong.

It is a telling result. After the change, Omny reported that people were more forgiving when a suggestion was wrong. Seeing where the AI's work stopped and theirs began is the likely reason.

## The trade-off: one extra click for clear accountability {#the-trade-off-this-case-is-really-about}

Tcules kept suggestions close to the work and made acceptance deliberate. Users got a useful check that fitted inside the task as it was.

The question to ask is which step carries the accountability, and whether the person taking it can tell. As AI writes more of what sellers publish, seeing who wrote each line becomes part of trusting the product.

Placing accountability in the right step is central to Tcules' work on [trust, control and recovery](/services/ai-product-ux/trust-control-recovery) and, more broadly, [AI product UX](/services/ai-product-ux).
