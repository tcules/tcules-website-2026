# When to hire a design system consultant, and how to judge one

By [Rakesh Chitroda](/insights/authors/rakesh-chitroda) · Updated 26 October 2026 · 13 min read

This article tells you when to hire a design system consultant and when to hold off. It sets out what a good one leaves behind, and which questions separate a strong provider from a weak one.

Hire a design system consultant when your team needs a decision it cannot make from the inside. The question may be what the system should own, why teams keep working around it, or how it should be run. Do not hire one to replace an owner. A good consultant leaves decisions, a working contribution path and adoption evidence your team can keep running.

The same test applies whether you call the help a consultant, a design system agency or a studio. What differs is how much building they do. What should not differ is what stays behind when they leave.

**Key takeaways**

- Hire a design system consultant when your team needs a decision it cannot make from the inside.
- Outside help cannot replace an owner. Someone inside has to keep the system once the work ends.
- Judge the work by the decisions it resolves and whether your team can run it afterwards. Component count proves neither.
- Consultants, agencies and AI agents are different kinds of help. Agents consume a design system without deciding what it owns.
- Ask how handover works before you ask about the portfolio.

## What does a design system consultant do? {#what-does-a-design-system-consultant-do}

A design system consultant helps a product team work out what its shared [design system](/glossary/design-system) should cover. They also shape how it works across design files and code, and how it will be run once they leave. Some advise only. Others also design and build components alongside your team.

The work usually lands in four areas: scope, architecture, governance and adoption. Designing your product's screens is a separate job. So is running the system day to day, which stays with an owner inside your team.

## Signs you need design system help, and the kind each calls for {#when-should-you-hire-a-design-system-consultant}

Short staffing is the challenge design system practitioners report most often. In zeroheight's [Design Systems Report 2026](https://report.zeroheight.com/), a survey of 147 design system practitioners, three in five teams said they were understaffed. In the same survey, 56% of respondents selected lack of resourcing or staffing as a challenge.

Capacity is a legitimate reason to bring someone in. It is a better reason when a specific problem comes with it, and an owner who will keep the result.

The table shows situations where outside help can pay for itself, and the kind of help each one calls for.

| What you are seeing | What kind of help it calls for | What to ask for first |
|---|---|---|
| Design, product and engineering all want a system, but for different reasons: speed, consistency, accessibility, brand | A scope decision | A record of what should be shared, what varies, what stays local and what is excluded, with an owner proposal |
| A library exists, but teams fork components or build around it | Diagnosis before any rebuild | A trace of one repeated component family through Figma, code and a live screen |
| Figma and code describe different systems, and design values (tokens) are synced by hand | Alignment across design and code | A map of which source governs which decision, and a token pipeline, which keeps design values in step with code automatically. zeroheight found only 40% of teams have any token pipeline |
| A named owner, too few people | Bounded build or repair capacity | A defined slice of work the owner accepts and can maintain afterwards |
| Several products or brands need to share foundations | An architecture decision | A choice between foundations only, a shared core, or a parent and child setup, with the trade-offs stated |
| AI coding tools now produce interface faster than anyone reviews it | Making the rules explicit and machine-readable | Semantic tokens, component intent, state definitions, code mappings and tests an agent can check its work against |

The common thread: each row names a decision or a bounded piece of work. "We need a design system" is not yet a brief.

## When an in-house team, or a different service, fits better {#when-a-consultant-is-the-wrong-answer}

Outside help does not fix a missing owner. Nathan Curtis made the point in 2016 in [A Design System Isn't a Project. It's a Product, Serving Products](https://articles.centercentre.com/a-design-system-isnt-a-project-its-a-product-serving-products/). A system's value arrives only when product teams ship features with it, and it rarely survives long without a sponsor funding it on purpose.

In 2024 he went further in [The Fallacy of Federated Design Systems](https://nathanacurtis.substack.com/p/the-fallacy-of-federated-design-systems-23b9a9a05542). He reversed his earlier preference for federated teams and observed that "virtually all productive output is delivered by central people."

A consultant is, for a while, those central people. When the work ends, someone inside has to be. If nobody will be, you are paying for a library that starts drifting the week the invoice closes. That is our reading of Curtis's argument applied to outside help. He makes no claim about consultants.

A consultant is also the wrong answer when:

- One feature keeps drifting between design and production. That points first to implementation acceptance. [Implementation QA](/services/design-engineering/implementation-qa) or a [design-to-code audit](/audits/design-to-code-audit) fits better.
- You need screens designed. Product design capacity and design system work are different jobs. A system consultant asked to design roadmap screens may deliver them slowly while the system work waits. Hire for the job you have.
- The honest answer is a smaller system. The responsible outcome may be foundations only, one shared pattern, or no new system at all. A good consultant will say so. One who assumes a full multi-platform system before looking is selling the build.

## What a design system consultant should deliver: a checklist {#what-should-a-good-design-system-consultant-deliver}

Judge the work by the decisions it resolves and whether your team can run it afterwards. A larger component count is not evidence of either. Use this table as a checklist when you scope the work and again when it ends.

| Deliverable | Why it matters | How to check it |
|---|---|---|
| A scope decision: shared, variable, local and excluded, with triggers to reopen it | Overreach makes the system a bottleneck; underreach leaves teams rebuilding the same behaviour | Every exclusion has a reason and a condition for reconsidering it |
| A system map | Shows where decisions live (Figma, code, documentation), who owns them and which products consume them | You can name the owner and release path for any shared component |
| An ownership and contribution path | A product team that needs a new state this week will fork if the system cannot answer in time | Each request type has a decision owner, required evidence and an expected response |
| Figma and code kept in step | Two unsynchronised sources of truth tend to drift apart | A stated rule for which source governs tokens, components and states, and token sync that does not depend on someone remembering |
| Components with states, accessibility and usage rules, proven in a real product | A component that has only lived in the library has not met real data, permissions or edge cases | At least one consuming product uses the output; a documentation site alone does not count |
| A migration and adoption sequence | Adoption happens in consuming products, one change at a time | An ordered list your team can work through, with blockers named |
| Adoption measures beyond component count | zeroheight found only 41% of teams measure adoption and 5% measure ROI | Versions in use, local forks and why they exist, time from request to release |
| Agent-readable context | Coding agents now read the system directly (more below) | Semantic tokens, component intent, code mappings and runnable interaction and accessibility tests |
| A handover your team can run | The work ends; the system should not | Your team can make the next release without the consultant in the room |

The vocabulary behind several of these rows, including ownership models and contribution routes, is in our definitions of [design system governance](/glossary/design-system-governance) and [design system drift](/glossary/design-system-drift).

## In-house team vs design system consultant, agency or AI agent {#consultant-agency-or-agent-which-kind-of-help}

Search is starting to blur these words. On 29 September 2026, Google autocomplete for "design system agen" suggested "design system agent" ahead of "design system agency." They are different kinds of help. A growing SaaS company may use all three, alongside its own team.

| Kind of help | Best for | What stays with you afterwards |
|---|---|---|
| **Your in-house team** | A named owner with time, and a system that fits one product or a few close ones | Everything, from the first day |
| **An independent consultant** | Advice on what to build and how to run it, while your own team does the building | Written-down choices and a way of running the system |
| **An agency or studio** | Building or repairing the system across Figma and code when your team lacks capacity | Working components, plus the reasoning behind them if handover is planned from the start |
| **AI agents** | Applying rules the system already makes explicit | Faster use of the system you have; deciding what it owns stays with people |

**An independent consultant or specialist** is strongest on strategy, architecture, governance and team practice. Build capacity can be limited, so expect your team to do most of the implementation.

**An agency or studio** adds design and frontend capacity to build or repair the system across Figma and code. The risk is the one above: the people who understood the system leave with the contract. Ask how handover works before you ask about the portfolio. Judge any provider, us included, on inspectable work and the deliverables above.

**AI agents** consume a design system. They do not decide what it should own. Figma's [MCP server](https://developers.figma.com/docs/figma-mcp-server/) passes variables, components and layout data to coding agents, and its Code Connect mappings help agents reuse your real components. Figma's own [argument for design systems in AI workflows](https://www.figma.com/blog/design-systems-ai-mcp/) compares an agent without design system context to a new engineer shipping before onboarding. Storybook's [MCP server](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/mcp/overview), still labelled a preview, lets agents read component documentation, write stories and run interaction and accessibility tests on what they generate.

These tools raise the value of explicit rules. An agent can apply a semantic token it can read. It cannot decide whether a new status belongs in the shared system or should stay local to one product, or who approves the exception. Those are still decisions for people, and they are the part of the work a consultant should leave written down.

zeroheight's 2026 survey also found only 10% of teams had AI built into their design system processes, with 46% experimenting, so most teams are early here.

## How to judge a design system consultant before and after you sign {#how-to-judge-the-work-before-and-after-you-sign}

The answers to these questions separate a partner who will leave you with a working system from one who will leave you with a library.

| Question to ask | A good answer sounds like | A warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| What will you look at first? | A real product path: a component family traced from Figma to code to a live screen | A Figma inventory or a maturity score on its own |
| What might you recommend we not build? | Scope options, including foundations only, with reasons | A full multi-platform system assumed before any evidence |
| Who owns this after you leave? | A request to meet the owner early and design around their capacity | Ongoing maintenance by the provider as the only plan |
| How will we know it is adopted? | Versions in consuming products, forks and their causes, time to decision | Component count, library opens or documentation visits |
| What will agents get from this? | Semantic tokens, component intent, code mappings, tests | "AI-ready" with nothing specific behind it |
| What does handover include? | A decision queue and migration sequence your team can run alone | A documentation site and a final presentation |

## What drives design system consultant cost {#what-drives-design-system-consultant-cost}

Five drivers set the price, and all of them are predictable. Two concern what is covered: how many products and platforms are in scope, and whether coded components are included or only Figma. Three concern the work itself. How many consuming products need migrating? How much accessibility repair is needed? Does governance have to be designed, or only documented?

A diagnosis of one component family is a much smaller commitment than a multi-platform build, and it can tell you whether the larger one is needed. Whoever you hire, ask for the work to be priced up front once the scope is agreed, so each driver is visible before you commit.

## Two design system decisions from our own work {#from-our-own-design-system-work}

Both cases are ours. Each shows a choice a good consultant should help you make before anything gets built.

### Benchmark Gensuite: shared components first, then one real feature

[Benchmark Gensuite](/case-studies/benchmark-gensuite-enterprise-suite-modernisation) was modernising an established environment, health, safety and sustainability suite. Its customers had built fast habits on the interface. The work began at the shared component layer, where one choice affects every application. It then moved into the scope selector, a control every application in the suite depends on.

We built the direction as coded prototypes in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, so Benchmark's engineers could judge whether it could be built while the choice was still open. The prototypes produced a component direction for the Bootstrap 5.3 migration.

The case also separates four jobs that tend to arrive bundled: a framework upgrade, a visual refresh, a change to how the product works and a design-system programme. Work out which of the four you are buying before you brief anyone.

### Auxentios: model the roles before the components

[Auxentios](/case-studies/auxentios-cpq-product-mvp-design), an industrial CPQ product, was expected to need more than 200 screens. We mapped six roles and the quotation they share before designing any component. Use cases were then mapped to screens, and repeated needs became components, with variants, design tokens, guidelines and layout rules.

A library built before that model would have standardised visual fragments and left the product logic open. A consultant worth hiring asks what the system is for before counting components.

## Design system consultant FAQs {#faq}

### When is it worth hiring a design system consultant?

It is worth it when your team faces a question it cannot answer from the inside, such as what the system should own or why teams keep working around it. It pays off when a named owner will keep the result. Capacity alone is a weaker reason, unless it comes with a bounded piece of work the owner accepts.

### What should a design system consultant deliver?

Work your team can run without them. That means a scope decision, a system map, a contribution path, a rule for which source governs Figma and code, and components proven in a real product. Add a migration sequence and a handover, and use the checklist above to check each one.

### Can our team manage a design system without outside help?

Yes, when someone owns it, has time for it and the scope fits one product or a few close ones. Outside help earns its fee when the team needs a decision it cannot make from the inside, or a bounded piece of build work its owner can maintain afterwards.

### How do I judge a design system consultant?

Ask what they will look at first, what they might advise you to leave unbuilt and who owns the system after they leave. Strong answers start from a real product path and plan the handover early. Weak ones lead with a component count, a maturity score or ongoing maintenance as the only plan.

### Do AI coding tools change when you need design system help?

They make written rules more valuable, because agents apply what they can read. Choosing what belongs in the shared system, and who approves an exception, remains a decision for people. If agents already produce interface faster than anyone reviews it, write the rules down before you hire for anything else.
