## Two-sided marketplace design: one booking, three points of view {#hard-parts}

Every marketplace turns on one thing changing hands: a booking, an order, a job or a lead. Before drawing screens, we pin it down: what it holds, when it becomes binding, what can still change and who acts next. Each side then looks at it with a different worry.

| Who | Before they commit | Once it's agreed |
|---|---|---|
| The buyer | "Can I trust this provider, and what exactly am I agreeing to?" | "Is it confirmed, and what happens if it goes wrong?" |
| The provider | "Is this request worth my time?" | "What changed, and when do I get paid?" |
| Your operations team | "Which matches look wrong?" | "Who is stuck, and what can I change for them?" |

### One booking that every side sees correctly

Buyer, provider and operations each see their own part of the same booking, so a change made by one shows up correctly for the others. Give each side its own app joined only by notifications, and your operations team ends up doing the joining by hand.

Research covers both sides and your own team, each interviewed separately. A marketplace that only listens to buyers loses its providers.

Example: CuramCare's clients and self-employed carers have an app each, with a web platform between them. After research with families on one side and carers on the other, the redesign took in all three at once.

One app at a time would have left the joins to chance, and the joins are where a marketplace succeeds or fails.

### Trust that people can check

Ratings and badges help. Clear availability, real prices, response times and plain cancellation terms help more, shown at the moment someone chooses. Behind them, your trust team needs screens for reports, suspensions and appeals, with a record of every action and its reason.

Rules now ask for some of this. In the EU, the Digital Services Act asks online marketplaces to collect and check details of the traders they host.

The Platform-to-Business Regulation asks for clarity on how rankings work, and DAC7 requires platforms to report sellers' income to tax authorities. Your legal team decides what applies; we design the seller onboarding, ranking explanations and reporting screens that meet it.

### Cancellations, changes, refunds and disputes

Cancelling, changing, refunding and disputing are stages of the booking, with rules both sides can read. Each stage has its own screen, so a problem can be worked out in the product.

Your operations lead writes each rule once, and the product says it three ways.

When a provider cancels late, the buyer sees what happens to the booking and who has been offered it instead. Before confirming, the provider sees that cancelling now counts as late and what that costs them. Your team sees the booking at risk, the hours left and a way to call in a replacement.

When the rule changes, all three views change together.

### Tools for the team that runs the market

Exceptions, quality checks and payment problems need screens of their own, with a record of who changed what. An admin panel full of one-off fixes usually means the main product is missing something.

The market's shape changes the tools. Service marketplaces sell time, such as a carer's visit or a plumber's call-out. Providers judge the platform by how quickly it pays them, and their tools run from a phone between jobs.

B2B marketplaces sell on account, with quotes, contract prices, approvals and payouts suppliers can predict, in a product that still feels as plain as a consumer store.

## Questions about marketplace product design {#faq}

### How is marketplace design different from ecommerce design?

In ecommerce, one business controls the offer, the price and the delivery. A marketplace coordinates independent providers who set some of their own terms, so trust, matching and disputes become product features. For single-seller stores, see [Commerce & Transactional Products](/expertise/commerce-transactional-products).

### What does a marketplace operations team need from the product?

A view of every exchange and its history, a list of the exceptions that need a person, and the authority to change things with a record of why. If your team fixes problems in spreadsheets or a database console today, we start there.

### What does marketplace trust and safety UX cover?

Buyers and providers see what they can check before they commit: availability, real prices, response times and cancellation terms. Your trust team gets screens for reports, suspensions and appeals, with a record of every action and its reason.

### Can AI help with marketplace matching?

Yes, when people can see why a match was suggested and can correct it. Tcules designs recommendations that show what they are based on, and your operations team can see which side acted, on whose instruction, when assistants book or quote for people.

### Can you take a marketplace from design into development?

Yes. Tcules leads with design, and for CuramCare our developers then built the frontend of the web platform and both apps. Backend work, integrations and payments sit with our Software Development service.

### Do you design B2B marketplaces as well as consumer ones?

Yes. B2B marketplaces add quotes, contract prices, approvals and payouts suppliers can predict. We research buyers, suppliers and your operations team separately, because each sees a different problem.
