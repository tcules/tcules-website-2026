# Taking a vibe-coded app to production: what to check, in order

By [Jatin Leuva](/insights/authors/jatin-leuva) · Updated 26 October 2026 · 15 min read

This article is for the founder or product lead holding a working app built with Lovable, Bolt, v0, Replit or Cursor. It shows what to check, in what order, to take a vibe-coded app to production, so it can carry real customers and the business you want to build on it.

A vibe-coded app is ready for real users when someone can explain four things. What does it store? Who can see each record? What happens when a step fails? Who fixes it when it breaks? The tool you used does not answer them for you. The prototype proved the idea. Production needs those answers.

**Key takeaways**

- A vibe-coded app is ready for real users when someone can say what it stores, who can see each record, what happens when a step fails and who fixes it.
- What production means depends on who uses the app next: you, testers, your team, paying customers or people with regulated data.
- The failures that reach the news start in the same few places: who can see which data, unsafe generated code, experiments touching real data, and the builder platform itself.
- You don't have to start again. Decide, part by part, whether to keep, specify, redesign or replace what the AI built.
- Seven steps take a prototype to production, from naming the next users to a small launch you can roll back.

## What is a vibe-coded app? {#what-is-a-vibe-coded-app}

A vibe-coded app is software built mostly by describing what you want to an AI builder and accepting the code it writes without reading it closely. The term describes how the app was made. On its own, it says little about whether the app is any good.

Three different things get called "the prototype", and each needs a different next step:

| What you have | Who has read the code | What it is good for |
|---|---|---|
| A vibe-coded app | Nobody, or only parts of it | Proving an idea, a demo you drive, a tool for yourself |
| A [coded prototype](/glossary/coded-prototype) | The designer or engineer who wrote it | Testing how the product should behave before the build |
| Production software | A named owner who can explain it | Carrying customers, their data and their money |

An app moves down this table as people read it, test it and take responsibility for it. The rest of this article is about that move.

## Is vibe coding production ready? {#is-vibe-coding-production-ready}

The answer depends on what "vibe coding" means, and the original meaning already contains it. Andrej Karpathy [coined the term in February 2025](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383) for coding where you "forget that the code even exists," and called it "not too bad for throwaway weekend projects."

Simon Willison later [drew the line more sharply](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/19/vibe-coding/): vibe coding is building software with a model without reviewing the code it writes. His golden rule for production-quality AI-assisted programming is that he will not commit any code he could not explain to someone else.

So the honest reading of the question is this. An app can start as vibe coding and reach production. It cannot stay vibe coding and be production software, because production means someone has read, tested and taken responsibility for what runs. The path to production is the path from "nobody has looked" to "someone owns this."

That does not mean starting again. Some AI-built software is already in real use. SaaStr founder Jason Lemkin [reports running a dozen or more vibe-coded apps with over 800,000 total uses](https://saastr.com/weve-built-12-vibe-coded-apps-used-800000-times-i-love-it-but-i-still-have-to-maintain-them-every-single-day/). In the same piece he says that at that scale they need maintenance every day. That is one operator's account and no benchmark. It shows both halves: it can work, and it becomes a job.

The tools will keep improving, and the line will move with them. As models write more of the code, and write it better, the scarce work shifts towards deciding what the product should do, for whom and what happens when it fails, then owning the result. That is product work, and it is what turns a fast prototype into software a business can stand behind.

## What a production-ready app needs, by who uses it next {#what-changes-when-real-users-arrive}

"Production" is set by who uses the app next and what they trust it with. The table below is our judgement of the minimum for each step up. Your domain or regulator may ask for more.

| Who uses it next | What has to be true before they do |
|---|---|
| Only you, or a demo you drive | Secrets such as API keys are not exposed in code or links you share. |
| Investors or testers on a guided walkthrough | Fake data is labelled as fake, nothing real is collected, and the demo path works on their device. |
| Your own team, as an internal tool | Access is limited to the team, data is backed up, and a named person can fix it when it breaks. |
| Paying customers or the public | Every record checks who is asking. Secrets stay on the server. Error, empty and recovery screens are designed on purpose. Real data lives apart from where you experiment. Errors are monitored, and someone owns the code. |
| Payments, health, children's or other regulated data | Everything above, plus a specialist security review and whatever your regulations require. Lovable's [own security documentation](https://docs.lovable.dev/features/security) says apps handling sensitive data should consider an additional professional security review. |

The first useful step is not technical. Write one sentence: who will use the app next, and what will they put into it? That sentence tells you which row you are in and how much of the rest of this article applies.

## Where vibe-coded apps break first: data access, security and product gaps {#where-do-vibe-coded-apps-break-first}

The failures that reach the news cluster in a few places. So do the quieter gaps that decide whether anyone keeps using the product.

**Who can see which data.** In 2025, two Replit employees [scanned 1,645 apps showcased by Lovable and found 170 that let anyone read user data](https://www.semafor.com/article/05/29/2025/the-hottest-new-vibe-coding-startup-lovable-is-a-sitting-duck-for-hackers), including names, emails, financial details and API keys. The [published vulnerability](https://mattpalmer.io/posts/2025/05/CVE-2025-48757/) traced it to missing or insufficient row level security, the database rules that decide which rows each user may touch. Supabase's [own documentation](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/postgres/row-level-security) is blunt: a table in an exposed schema without those rules is readable and writable by any role with a grant on it. The app worked in the demo because the demo never had a second, hostile user.

**Generated code that works but is unsafe.** Veracode tested code from more than 100 large language models on a benchmark of coding tasks. It found that [45% of samples introduced a security flaw from the OWASP Top 10](https://www.veracode.com/blog/genai-code-security-report/), with no improvement from newer or larger models. That measures benchmark tasks, not deployed apps, so treat it as a reason to review your own app.

**Experiments touching real data.** In July 2025 a Replit agent [deleted a live production database during a code freeze](https://fortune.com/2025/07/23/ai-coding-tool-replit-wiped-database-called-it-a-catastrophic-failure/) while Lemkin was building with it. The data was later recovered, and Replit responded by separating development and production databases automatically. The lesson applies to any tool: the place where you try things must not be the place where customers' records live.

**The platform itself.** Your builder is part of your risk surface. In April 2026 The Next Web [reported an authorisation flaw in Lovable's own API](https://thenextweb.com/news/lovable-vibe-coding-security-crisis-exposed) that exposed source code and database credentials from projects created before November 2025. You cannot fix the platform. You can know where your data and credentials live, and rotate them when something like this is disclosed.

**The product decisions nobody asked for.** Engineering checklists cover secrets, authentication, error handling, testing, automated builds and hosting. The product decisions behind those paths are a different layer, and that is where a builder tool fills silence with plausible defaults.

What does a new account see before it has any data? What happens when someone closes the tab halfway through a task, or two people edit the same record? What can an admin do that a member cannot? A screen that says "Invitation sent" may not be connected to any email service at all.

Accessibility belongs here too. Researchers report that large language models generating web interfaces [frequently violate the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13914). Those failures can shut out keyboard and screen-reader users while the demo looks fine.

None of these are exotic. They are what a demo does not need and a product cannot do without.

## How to make your vibe-coded app production ready, in seven steps {#the-path-from-prototype-to-production}

Work through these in order. Each step has a check that tells you it is done. Depending on your row in the table above, some steps take one afternoon of your own time and some need another person.

| Step | Done when |
|---|---|
| 1. Name the next users and what they will trust the app with. | One sentence places you in a row of the table above, and everyone involved agrees with it. |
| 2. Walk one real workflow and label each step demonstrated, simulated or unknown. | You know which screens do real work and which only look finished. Our free [Prototype Readiness Checklist](/tools/prototype-readiness-checklist) gives you a structure for this. |
| 3. Close access and data gaps first. | Logged out, and logged in as a second user, you cannot see or change anyone else's records. No secret key ships to the browser. |
| 4. Separate experiments from real data, and back it up. | Your builder or agent cannot reach production data, and you have restored a backup at least once. |
| 5. Decide the situations the demo never needed. | Empty, loading, error, interrupted work, a second user, revoked access, a phone screen and keyboard-only use each have a deliberate answer. |
| 6. Put someone who can explain the code in charge of it. | A named person can say what each critical part does, and has decided what to keep, specify, redesign or replace. |
| 7. Launch small, with a way to see failures and a way back. | Errors reach someone who acts on them, and you can roll back a bad release. |

Step 6 is easy to skip, because the app appears to run itself. Willison's rule and Lemkin's daily maintenance point the same way: once real people rely on the app, it needs an owner who understands it.

Deployment sits inside steps 3, 4 and 7. Most builders will publish the app for you in a click, which is fine for a demo. For real users, the same click needs three things behind it: a separate place to experiment, secret keys held on the server, and a release you have practised rolling back.

## Should you keep, specify, redesign or replace what the AI built? {#should-you-keep-harden-or-rebuild-what-the-ai-built}

The UK government's service manual [advises against copying prototype code straight into production](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/making-prototypes). Prototype code does not need to meet the same standards as production code. That is sound advice. It does not mean throwing the prototype away. Decide part by part, on evidence:

| Decision | When it fits |
|---|---|
| Keep | The behaviour is right, someone can explain the code, and it already meets the conditions of your row in the table above. |
| Specify | The behaviour is right, but the rules behind it were never written down: access rules, error handling or what gets stored. Writing them down gives the engineering work something to meet. |
| Redesign | The code may be fine, but the product behaviour was a default nobody chose, such as what happens on failure or who can do what. |
| Replace | The structure blocks the next version, for example a data model that cannot express the roles or records you now need. |

The interaction your users liked can be worth keeping even when the code underneath is not. Separate the product question from the code question before anyone quotes a rewrite.

## Vibe-coded app maintenance and the technical debt to plan for {#vibe-coded-app-maintenance-and-technical-debt}

Launch is when the maintenance starts. Lemkin's account above is the plain version: apps that work still need someone every day.

Technical debt in a vibe-coded app often takes a few recognisable forms:

- the same logic written several times, slightly differently, because each prompt solved its own problem;
- libraries the builder added that nobody chose and nobody updates;
- no automated tests, so every change is checked by clicking through;
- rules that live only in prompts or chat history, with no record of why the code does what it does.

Plan for three things before the first paying customer. Name an owner for the code. Put tests on the workflows customers pay for. Set a routine for updating libraries and rotating secrets.

Some debt is fine to carry. A prototype that proved the idea has done its job, and paying the debt down can wait until a part blocks the next version. Keep a short written list of the rules the app enforces, so the next prompt or the next engineer leaves them intact.

## From coded demo to live module: what our ConnectX work showed {#from-coded-demo-to-live-module}

Our clearest first-hand example of this path is [ConnectX](/case-studies/ai-native-building-energy-product-connectx), a building-energy product. Tcules shaped it around eight scenarios and proved it in a coded demo running on sample data. We then took that demo through to a production-ready module, live since late 2025.

Two lessons from that work apply to any prototype, however it was built.

**Write down what each part must do.** Each ConnectX scenario named the request that starts it, the data required, the interface modules, the twin's behaviour and the next action available. The build had something definite to meet.

**Sample data hides the hard part.** A demo on sample data shows what the product should do. It also shows how much a live version still has to connect.

## How we sort a prototype before launch {#five-states}

Before launch, we sort each important area of a prototype into one of five states:

| State | What it means before launch |
|---|---|
| **Proven** | It worked under the conditions we tested |
| **Assumed** | The team believes it works, and nobody has checked |
| **Missing** | A real product needs it, and it is absent |
| **Parked** | A known gap, left for later on purpose |
| **For a specialist** | Security, infrastructure or compliance work that needs a qualified owner |

Most of the work before launch is moving items out of Assumed, one way or the other.

## Who can help take your app to production? Options compared {#who-can-help-with-which-part}

Two questions sit under "is it ready?": is the product behaviour decided, and is the code safe to build on. Different kinds of help answer different parts of them.

| Route | Best for | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| **Work through it yourself** with the free checklist | An internal tool or a guided demo, where the stakes are low and you know the app well | Code you cannot read stays unreviewed |
| **A freelance developer** | A small app whose behaviour is already decided and needs fixes and a safe deployment | Questions about roles and failure screens tend to get answered in code, by default |
| **A development company** | A decided product that needs engineering capacity to harden it or rebuild parts | Behaviour nobody specified gets built as it stands |
| **A security specialist** | Payments, health, children's or other regulated data | Product behaviour and maintenance sit with someone else |
| **A design-led review**, such as ours | An app heading for paying customers whose behaviour was never decided on purpose | Engineers, yours or ours, still have to do the work it plans |
| **A fresh build** by an engineering team | A data model or structure that blocks the next version | What users liked can be lost unless the interaction is written down first |

Our [Prototype Readiness Review](/audits/prototype-readiness-review) covers both. A senior designer walks your running app against the next version you intend to build. They check roles, screen conditions such as empty and error, recovery, and which parts of the demo were real. With access to the repository, an engineer reads the code alongside it. You get a map of what to keep, specify, redesign or replace, and an ordered plan for the work before launch. A public link or screen recording is enough to start the product side.

Anything that needs a security, infrastructure or compliance specialist is named in the plan, with an owner.

When the review is done, your engineers can work from the plan, or Tcules can take the first release into working software. See [Design-led MVP Development](/services/mvp-development).

## Vibe-coded app to production: questions founders ask {#faq}

### Is my vibe-coded app ready for real users?

It is ready when someone can say what it stores, who can see each record, what happens when a step fails and who fixes it. How much more you need depends on who uses it next, from a guided demo to paying customers with regulated data. The table of next users above sets the minimum for each.

### Are vibe-coded apps secure?

Some are, and many are exposed in the same few ways. The best-documented gap so far is missing database access rules, which let one user read another's records. Treat generated code as unreviewed until someone has checked access, secrets and data separation, and get a specialist security review before you handle payments, health or other regulated data.

### Do I have to rebuild a vibe-coded app from scratch?

Rarely in full. Decide part by part whether to keep, specify, redesign or replace what the AI built, and replace only where the structure blocks the next version. The interaction your users liked is often worth keeping even when the code underneath is rewritten.

### How do I deploy a vibe-coded app safely?

Publishing from the builder is fine for a demo. Before real users arrive, keep experiments and real data apart, hold secret keys on the server and restore a backup at least once. Send errors to someone who acts on them, and make sure you can roll back a bad release.

### Can the AI builder fix its own security problems?

It can fix the problems you ask it to look for, and it can introduce new ones while it does. Someone still has to know which questions to ask, read the change and test it as a second, hostile user. That is Willison's rule in practice: commit only code someone can explain.

### What does it cost to make a vibe-coded app production ready?

It depends on who uses the app next, how many workflows customers rely on and how much of the code you keep. A focused review of one core workflow is the smallest useful step. The work after it is priced up front once the scope is agreed, as [How We Work](/how-we-work) explains.
